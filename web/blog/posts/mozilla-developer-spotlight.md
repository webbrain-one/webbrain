## What the spotlight covers

The post leads with the privacy argument behind WebBrain. The extension runs local LLMs, so page content and the personal details you share stay on your device instead of traveling to a cloud provider. Mozilla notes that WebBrain is designed for machines with at least 16 GB of RAM or a GPU with 16 GB of VRAM, and points readers to the [GitHub repo](https://github.com/webbrain-one/webbrain) for the full privacy documentation.

> When you ask a cloud provider anything, it isn't end-to-end encrypted and the things you share can be very intimate and personal. That's bad. Health data, financial, private life.
>
> *Emre Sokullu, in Mozilla's Developer Spotlight*

From there, Scott walks through what WebBrain does on an ordinary page. It can summarize a long article, analyze images, offer to download media on video sites, and take on repetitive page tasks such as GitHub and social media interactions. It also learns your preferred details for forms, like a username or email address, and keeps that memory local too.

Mozilla also mentions the free tier and the option to connect paid AI accounts you already use. And it gives Apocalypse Mode a proper introduction: an offline LLM chat paired with offline Wikipedia and medical sources, built for emergencies, internet outages, and places where the network simply isn't there.

## Why this matters

When we started building WebBrain, we wanted to challenge the assumption that powerful AI agents have to operate in the cloud. We believe the future of AI browsing can be open source, local-first, and privacy-conscious without compromising what agents can accomplish.

Seeing Mozilla describe that idea to its own add-ons community is a proud moment for the project. It also puts the Firefox extension in front of people who care about the open web, which is exactly where we want to be.

Huge thanks to Scott and the Mozilla team for the feature, and to everyone contributing to WebBrain.
